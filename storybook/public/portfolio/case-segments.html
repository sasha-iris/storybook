Customer segments: the best-looking rows were the broken ones

The brief was one sentence. The research found that five of the forty segments returned the entire customer base. Because they carried the most revenue, the page ranked them first.

Role
Research, interaction design, prototype, review facilitation
Brief
“Have a look at our customer segments page and take a stab at an updated UI”
Data
All 40 segments are the product's real ones, numbers untouched
Output
Two variants, six pages, reviewed by the product owner point by point

Starting without a spec

There was no written requirement. The live page became the specification, so I inventoried it from the DOM. Every figure in the table below was read off the running product.

The finding that changed the brief

Against a customer base of 35,590, five segments reported between 31,000 and 35,600 customers. Three of them returned byte-identical numbers: 35,590 customers, 75,437 orders, $3,745,567 in net sales. Their geography filter existed but had never been given a value, so it filtered nothing.

In the live product these look like the best segments on the page, because they have the biggest revenue. Seven of the forty had “California” in the name, one of them spelled Calfirnoia. Eight had never been calculated at all; twenty-two were more than thirty days stale.

That turned a visual refresh into a different question: how does a list tell you which of its rows you can trust, without becoming a wall of error states?

Decisions

Ordering

Lead with what cannot be trusted, not with what earns most

The live product's implicit ranking put the broken segments on top because revenue put them there. An early version of my redesign reproduced that by sorting on customer count. The default now leads with the rows whose numbers cannot be relied on.

Refused

Filling the empty rows with plausible numbers

Eight segments had never run. A prototype is read as a specification, so inventing numbers for them would have quietly promised a calculation that does not exist. They read “no data”. In the same pass I removed a condition catalogue that had been inventing SKUs, product families, tags and a country appearing nowhere in the dataset, and an estimate panel that named four customers and printed their spend on an empty form.

Removed

“% of company revenue”, because the column invited an addition it could not survive

Segments overlap. Across the forty, those percentages sum to 855%. A column that looks like a share of something, and cannot be added up, will be added up by someone.

Controls

A switch has to add something when it is on

A “Full definitions” toggle turned a row from “no effective filter, every customer matches” into “geography (no criteria set)”. That is not more detail, it is a different reading of the same fact, and the control had no visible on-state at all. It became a two-option control, Summary or Conditions, where picking one makes it clear you are picking a view.

Measurement over opinion

I built a layout, measured it, deleted it — and withdrew my own review note

I had argued in a review that putting conditions in their own column gives the eye a rail to read down. Building both and measuring forty rows at 1600px said otherwise: both layouts placed every condition list at an identical left edge, standard deviation 0px. The column version lost on five of seven measures: 235px of horizontal travel from name to condition, 44 wrapped conditions against none, and 898px more table height. The argument was wrong, so it went, and the redesign proposal it supported went with it.

Reversal

Restoring the order window I had cut

The first version dropped the time window from the condition row for tidiness, which made two segments with identical conditions and different windows impossible to tell apart. Two segments that differ only by their window have to stay tellable apart.

Colour

One visual token, one meaning

Purple marked segment type on the list, and had been recoloured by performance band on the detail page, so it meant two things inside one page family. Three purples became two, with the split stated: brand for interactive chrome, a second for data. A “broken” share bar had been coloured with the chart palette's trend-down pink, which is not a semantic error colour. The library has no danger variant for progress bars, and that gap is now on the library's list.

Restraint

Withholding a rank, and refusing a confidence score

A segment that returns nobody gets no rank, because last place reads as a verdict on a segment that was simply never run. When the reviewer asked for a confidence level on the plain-language parsing, I did not invent a percentage. The screen shows its working instead: each phrase, the condition it became, and the words it passed over.

The review

Before the product owner saw it, the prototype went through three passes: business analysis against the brief, a usability and design-system review, and risk-based QA in the running page. Together they closed 48 defects. The QA round alone logged 13, four of them accessibility.

Accessibility was measured, not assumed. Both pages at 375, 1120, 1280 and 1440 finished at zero contrast failures, zero controls under the 24px target minimum, zero unnamed controls, no horizontal overflow and a clean console. Getting there meant fixing a separator at 1.68:1, an em-dash placeholder at 2.54:1, a 20px row link under the 24px minimum, a screen-reader-only label pushing the document 38px sideways, and an inline grid rule holding the page 29px wider than a phone.

The point left open is not a design question: four segment templates exist and every value in them is real, but deciding which templates the product should ship is the owner's call, not a layout's.

One instruction from that call sent me back through the page. He did not want the product's data problems in the headline, because he expects future data to have its own problems. So the three defect counts left the summary strip, the red banner became one line of ordinary text, the chart lost its eight red bars, and a filter that would return nothing is dimmed and inert instead of shouting. The information is still reachable. It stopped being the first thing the page says about itself.

What I would still fix

The comparison page answers a different question than the one the live comparison answers. I wrote that into the handoff as the biggest coverage gap in the prototype, rather than leave it to be discovered in build.
